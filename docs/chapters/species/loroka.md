# Loroka

![Loroka](../../assets/img/species-loroka.jpg){ .fh-portrait }

The old blood of the Theymoron highlands — farmers, drovers and terrace-workers who worked these valleys long before the Marche had a name. Once the plurality of a conquered people, drawn heavily from the Sira low caste of the Empire, they are now the plurality of a free city: soldiers when the Front calls for it, but merchants, roofers and rice-growers the rest of the year.

Three in ten of Theymoron are Loroka, and a larger share again of the Marche beyond the walls. That makes them the single largest people in the only kingdom on this continent that has never paid the Empire tribute — a fact they mention less often than you would expect, and never first.

The reticence has a reason. The Loroka came out of the Empire, and mostly out of its bottom caste: Sira, sent to work ground that belonged to someone else. What they built afterwards is the terraced rice that feeds a city at two and a half thousand metres, which took generations and is still being extended. Loroka households measure a family by which terrace it cut. A grandfather who added forty metres of wall is spoken of the way other peoples speak of a battle.

The Front is the other half of the year. Theymoron's refusal of tribute has cost it thirty years of open war and seventy of the kind that stops and starts, and the Loroka are the mass of the levies that have made the refusal hold. They are not a warrior caste and dislike being described as one — a Loroka soldier is a roofer who will be back on the roofs in spring, and the whole arrangement depends on him surviving to get there. He generally does. Loroka do not stop when a thing has gone badly wrong, and the Empire has spent a hundred years discovering how expensive that is.

Their orc kin to the south get the reputation for ferocity. The Loroka get the harvest in. Ask one which he would rather have and he will not understand the question — the point of the war is the terraces, and a people who forget that has lost the war whatever the maps say.
